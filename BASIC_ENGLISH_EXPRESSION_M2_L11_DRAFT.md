# Module 2 · Lesson 11 — Time & Sequence

> **Draft status:** first complete teaching-content draft, based on *The ABC of Basic English*, printed pp.258–260, and the accepted C11 teaching flow. The source's different contexts are kept separate. Closed exercises have answer keys. Actual learner time and feedback are unmeasured; this draft is not user-tested or App-integrated.

## What this lesson teaches

This is not a lesson on reading clocks, naming dates, counting time or learning basic tense forms. Those belong to the existing foundations.

This lesson teaches the learner to identify the time relation in a sentence:

- a situation was true during an earlier period;
- an event happened before the present point;
- an account covers events through the present point;
- an expected event has not happened by now;
- an event is related to an earlier starting point;
- an action follows another, or the same action happens on the next occasion;
- a state or relationship continues through time.

The same ordinary-English word can express different relations. The whole sentence decides which meaning is intended.

## 1. once and ago — an earlier period

### once

The source gives two meanings:

- a single occurrence — Module 2 · Lesson 10;
- something that was true during an earlier period — this lesson.

Here, once places a situation in the past. It does not count how many times an event happened.

### ago

For its long-period example, the source gives the Basic expression **a long time back**. It also gives another phrase referring to earlier days.

Keep the scope of the example. The source passage does not establish a universal formula for every numbered duration followed by ago.

## 2. already, so far, yet and and yet

These uses relate an event or state to the present point, but they are not interchangeable.

### already

The source gives two contexts:

- An event that has already taken place happened before the present point.
- When a person is already at the expected place, the person is there now.

The first is about when an event happened; the second is about a person's present location. Do not use the event meaning to explain the location example.

### so far / up to now

An account of events may cover what has happened from an earlier period through the present point. The source expresses this with **so far** and **up to now**.

This describes the time covered by the account. It is not simply another way to say that one event happened already.

### yet in a negative sentence

If an expected person or event is not present by the current point, the meaning is that the expected arrival or event has still not happened.

The relation is **not completed by now**.

### and yet as a joining statement

The source separately explains **and yet** as a joining expression meaning **at the same time**.

Keep the two uses apart:

- negative yet — an expected event has not happened by now;
- the joining use — two statements hold together.

## 3. since — a time relation or a reason

The source gives three contexts.

### A. since with a past-year reference

The source's example concerns friends who have not been seen from a past-year starting point. It explains the whole idea as not having seen them for a long time.

That is a paraphrase of the complete sentence. It does not make since a universal substitute for for a long time.

### B. since then

When the source describes events that have taken place since a particular earlier point, it explains that those events took place **after then**.

The earlier point is the reference; the sentence identifies events that happened later than it.

### C. since giving a reason

In the source's example about lateness and going to bed, since gives a reason. Its Basic expression is **because**.

This is not a time meaning. It belongs to the sentence-relation foundation and is included here to prevent confusion with the time uses.

## 4. next — sequence, repetition or position

The source gives three different uses.

### A. The following action

When a sentence asks what will be done next, it refers to the action that follows the present one in the sequence.

### B. The next occasion of the same action

When a sentence says that a person next does the same thing, it means that the person repeats that action on the next occasion. It does not mean that the person chooses a different action in a list.

### C. Spatial position

When next is used to say that one house is beside another, the source describes the first house as the closest to the reference house.

This is spatial adjacency, not time or sequence. It cross-links to Module 2 · Lesson 4 — Movement & Position.

## 5. always — general continuation, frequency or future continuation

The source gives several contexts:

- The general meaning is **at all times**.
- In some connections, the intended meaning may be that something happens often.
- When the future is specially in view, as in a statement about remaining someone's friend, the source says **for ever** is a better way of putting it.
- The source also notes that descriptions meaning unchanged or continuing without an end are right in other connections.

These are context-dependent choices. Do not teach always as one fixed frequency word, and do not use the future expression as a universal replacement.

## 6. How to choose

Ask these questions before choosing an expression:

1. Is the sentence placing a situation in an earlier period, or describing what is true now?
2. Is an event completed before now, or is a person simply present at a place?
3. Does the account cover events through the present point?
4. Is the expected event still outstanding, or is yet joining two statements?
5. Does since refer to a past point, a later event, or a reason?
6. Does next identify the following action, a repeated action, or a spatial relation?
7. Does always mean continuous truth, frequent occurrence in this context, or future continuation?

Do not choose a paraphrase from the target word alone. Identify the relation in the whole sentence.

## Practice

### A. Earlier time or present location

Choose the meaning that matches the context.

1. Something was once true. What time relation is intended?
   - A. It was true during an earlier period.
   - B. It must have happened only one time.
   - C. It is true at every moment.

2. A thing was done once, and the sentence counts its occurrences. Which lesson handles this meaning?
   - A. Module 2 · Lesson 10 — Quantity, Amount & Degree.
   - B. Module 2 · Lesson 11 — Time & Sequence.

3. The source's long-period ago example uses which Basic expression?
   - A. a long time back.
   - B. after then.
   - C. at all times.

4. An event has already taken place. What is the intended relation?
   - A. The event happened before the present point.
   - B. The event has not happened by now.
   - C. The event will happen on the next occasion.

5. A person is already present at the expected place. What does the sentence tell us?
   - A. The person is currently at that place.
   - B. The person will go there next.
   - C. The person is absent.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — This is the past-time sense of once; it does not count occurrences.
2. A — C10 handles one occurrence; C11 handles a situation that was true in the past.
3. A — This is the source's Basic expression for its long-period example.
4. A — Already describes the event as having taken place before the present point.
5. A — This is the source's separate present-location context.

### B. Events through now, negative yet and the joining use

1. An account includes events from an earlier period through the present point. Which description matches the source?
   - A. An account of what has taken place so far / up to now.
   - B. An account only of what will happen later.
   - C. An account of one event only.

2. A person is expected at a place but has not arrived by the present point. Which relation is intended?
   - A. The person is still absent.
   - B. The person is already present.
   - C. The person will arrive on the next occasion.

3. The source's negative-yet example says that the person has not come by now. What does this test?
   - A. A past event.
   - B. An event not completed by the present point.
   - C. A repeated action.

4. The joining use of and yet has which meaning?
   - A. Two statements hold at the same time.
   - B. An event has not happened by now.
   - C. An action follows another.

5. Are negative yet and the joining use of yet the same?
   - A. Yes. Both always mean that an event has not happened.
   - B. No. One concerns an outstanding event; the other joins statements that hold together.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source uses this description for an account covering events through the present point.
2. A — Negative yet describes the expected arrival as not having happened by now.
3. B — The event remains outstanding at the present reference point.
4. A — The joining use means that both statements hold together.
5. B — The two contexts must not be collapsed into one translation.

### C. since — time or reason

1. The source's example concerns friends not seen from a past-year starting point and paraphrases the whole idea as not having seen them for a long time. What must the learner remember?
   - A. This explains the whole example; it does not make since a universal substitute for for a long time.
   - B. Since always means for a long time.
   - C. Since always gives a reason.

2. Events that have taken place since a particular earlier point are described as taking place:
   - A. before that point.
   - B. after that point.
   - C. at the same moment as that point.

3. In the source's example about it being late and going to bed, since gives:
   - A. a reason.
   - B. a repeated action.
   - C. a nearby position.

4. Which context uses since as a reason rather than a time relation?
   - A. Events after a particular earlier point.
   - B. Friends not seen from a past-year starting point.
   - C. The statement that it is late is used to explain the decision to go to bed.

5. Should every use of since be interpreted as a time starting point?
   - A. Yes.
   - B. No. The source also uses it to give a reason.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source paraphrases the complete friend example. It does not establish one fixed replacement for every since sentence.
2. B — The earlier point is the reference; the events happened later.
3. A — In this context since means because.
4. C — The statement about lateness gives the reason for the action.
5. B — The learner must distinguish time from reason.

### D. next — sequence, repetition or position

1. The sentence asks what will be done next. Which relation is intended?
   - A. The action that follows in the sequence.
   - B. The same action happened in the past.
   - C. A place is beside another place.

2. The sentence says that a person next performs the same action. What does next indicate?
   - A. The next occasion on which the same action is repeated.
   - B. A different action that must happen first.
   - C. A place beside the person.

3. One house is described as next to another. Which relation is intended?
   - A. Time sequence.
   - B. Spatial closeness.
   - C. Repetition.

4. Which use of next belongs to Module 2 · Lesson 4 rather than this lesson's time/sequence teaching?
   - A. The action that follows.
   - B. The next occasion of the same action.
   - C. The spatial relation between two houses.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source describes the action that follows the present one.
2. A — Here next marks the next occurrence of the same action.
3. B — This is a spatial relation.
4. C — Spatial adjacency cross-links to Movement & Position.

### E. always — general meaning or a particular context

1. What is the general meaning of always given by the source?
   - A. At all times.
   - B. Only once.
   - C. After a particular event.

2. In some connections, what other sense may be intended?
   - A. Often.
   - B. Never.
   - C. Only in the past.

3. When a future relationship is specially in view, what expression does the source recommend for the example about remaining someone's friend?
   - A. for ever.
   - B. after then.
   - C. a long time back.

4. Does the source say that for ever, unchanged, and endless are universal replacements for always?
   - A. Yes.
   - B. No. The source attaches them to particular contexts; the latter descriptions suit other connections.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — This is the general meaning.
2. A — The source says that an often/frequent sense may fit some connections.
3. A — For a future relationship, the source recommends this as a better way of putting it.
4. B — These expressions are context-dependent, not interchangeable in every sentence.

### F. Keep neighbouring lessons and foundations separate

1. A sentence means that an action happened one time only. Which lesson is its primary home?

   **Answer:** Module 2 · Lesson 10 — Quantity, Amount & Degree.

2. A sentence means that something was true during an earlier period. Which lesson is its primary home?

   **Answer:** Module 2 · Lesson 11 — Time & Sequence.

3. The word next describes a place beside another place. Which lesson provides the spatial foundation?

   **Answer:** Module 2 · Lesson 4 — Movement & Position.

4. Since gives a reason rather than a time relation. Where does that sentence relation belong?

   **Answer:** The existing sentence-relation foundation. C11 identifies the contrast but does not create a separate reason lesson.

5. A learner needs to name a date, read a clock or count a period of time. Is that the main task of C11?

   **Answer:** No. Those belong to the existing number/time foundation. C11 focuses on the additional meanings and context distinctions in the source.

**Suggested answers**

### H. Independent construction

Choose two situations and explain the time relation before constructing a Basic expression:

- something was true during an earlier period;
- an event happened before the present point;
- an account covers events through now;
- an expected event has not happened by now;
- two statements hold together;
- an event happened after a stated earlier point;
- a sentence gives a reason;
- an action follows another action;
- the same action happens on the next occasion;
- something continues through time;
- something happens often in a particular context;
- a future relationship is intended to continue without an end.

First identify the relation. Then choose the expression that preserves it. Finally, explain why a neighbouring meaning does not fit.
