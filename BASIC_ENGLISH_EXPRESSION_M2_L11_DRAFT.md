# Module 2 · Lesson 11 — Time & Sequence

> **Draft status:** first complete teaching-content draft, based on *The ABC of Basic English*, printed pp.258–260, and the accepted C11 teaching flow. It keeps the source's different contexts separate. The closed exercises have answer keys; learner time and learner feedback have not yet been measured. This draft is not user-tested or App-integrated.

## What this lesson teaches

This lesson is not about reading a clock, naming dates, counting time, or teaching the basic tense foundation. It teaches how to identify the time relation that an ordinary-English sentence is expressing.

The learner must distinguish:

1. a past period;
2. an event that happened before the present point;
3. what has happened through the present point;
4. something that has not happened by the present point;
5. a time relation beginning from an earlier point;
6. what follows in a sequence or happens on the next occasion;
7. a condition that continues through time.

The same word can express different relations. The context, not the word alone, decides which meaning is intended.

## 1. once and ago — referring to an earlier time

### once: the past-time meaning

The source gives two meanings for once:

- one occurrence only — taught in Module 2 · Lesson 10;
- something was true during an earlier period — taught here.

For this lesson, once means that the situation was true at some time in the past. It does not tell us that the event happened only one time.

**Keep the two lessons separate:**

- C10: count the number of occurrences;
- C11: place a situation in an earlier period.

### ago: a long period before now

For its long-time example, the source gives a Basic expression meaning a long time back. It also gives a phrase referring to earlier days.

Do not turn this one example into a universal formula for every expression such as three days ago or five years ago. The source passage does not establish that general rule.

## 2. already, so far, yet and and yet

These expressions relate an event or state to the present point, but they do not all mean the same thing.

### already: an earlier event or a present location

The source gives two different contexts:

- An event that has already taken place happened before the present point.
- A person who has already arrived at a place is there now.

The first describes when an event happened. The second describes the person's present location. Do not use the event paraphrase for the location example.

### so far / up to now: the period through the present

When giving an account of events, the source describes the account as covering what has taken place from the earlier period through the present point.

This is a time boundary: the account includes events up to now. It is not simply another way to say that one event happened already.

### yet in a negative sentence

If a person is not at the expected place yet, the meaning is that the person is still absent at the present point. The source also explains the idea as the person not having come by now.

The important relation is **not completed by the present point**.

### and yet as a joining statement

The source separately explains and yet as a joining expression meaning that two statements are true at the same time.

Do not confuse:

- negative yet — an expected event has not happened by now;
- and yet — two facts hold at the same time.

The word yet is not being used in the same way in these two contexts.

## 3. since — a time relation or a reason

The source gives three contexts. They must remain separate.

### A. since with a past-year reference

The source gives an example about friends who have not been seen since a past year. It then explains the whole idea as not having seen them for a long time.

This is a paraphrase of the complete sentence. Do not memorize since = for a long time as a universal replacement.

### B. since then

When the source describes events that have taken place since a particular earlier point, it explains that those events took place after that point.

The earlier point is the reference. The sentence tells us which events happened later than it.

### C. since giving a reason

In the source's example about it being late and going to bed, since gives the reason. Its meaning is because.

This is not a time expression. It belongs to the sentence-relation foundation and is included here only so that the learner does not mistake a reason for a time starting point.

## 4. next — sequence, repetition or position

The source gives three different uses.

### A. What happens next

When the sentence asks what will be done next, it refers to the action that follows the present one in the sequence. The source expresses this with the idea of what happens after this.

### B. When an action happens next

When the sentence says that a person next does the same action, it refers to doing that action again on the next occasion.

This is not merely the next different action in a list. It is the next occurrence of the same action.

### C. next to — spatial position

When one house is next to another, the source describes it as the house nearest to the reference house.

This is spatial adjacency, not time or sequence. It cross-links to Module 2 · Lesson 4 — Movement & Position.

## 5. always — all times, frequency or future continuation

The source gives several contexts:

- The general meaning is that something is true at all times.
- In some connections, the intended meaning may be that something happens often.
- When the future is specially in view, as in a statement about remaining someone's friend, the source says for ever is a better way of putting it.
- The source also notes that descriptions meaning unchanged or continuing without an end are right in other connections.

These are context-dependent choices. Do not teach always as one fixed frequency word, and do not treat for ever or the other descriptions as universal replacements.

## 6. How to choose the time relation

When you meet one of these words, ask:

1. Is the sentence placing something in an earlier period, or describing what is true now?
2. Is an event already completed, or is a person simply present at a place?
3. Does the account cover events through the present point?
4. Is the expected event still outstanding, or is and yet joining two statements?
5. Does since refer to an earlier time, a later event after that time, or a reason?
6. Does next identify the following action, the next occurrence of the same action, or a nearby place?
7. Does always mean at all times, often in this context, or future continuation?

Do not choose a Basic expression from the target word alone. First identify the relation in the whole sentence.

## Practice

### A. Earlier time or present location

Choose the meaning that matches the context.

1. The sentence says that something was once true. What time relation is intended?
   - A. It was true during an earlier period.
   - B. It must have happened only one time.
   - C. It is true at every moment.

2. A thing was done once, and the sentence is counting its occurrences. Which lesson handles this meaning?
   - A. Module 2 · Lesson 10 — Quantity, Amount & Degree.
   - B. Module 2 · Lesson 11 — Time & Sequence.

3. The source's long-time example uses ago. Which Basic expression does it give?
   - A. a long time back.
   - B. after then.
   - C. at all times.

4. An event has already taken place. What is the intended relation?
   - A. The event happened before the present point.
   - B. The event has not happened by now.
   - C. The event will happen on the next occasion.

5. A person has already arrived at the place. What does the sentence tell us?
   - A. The person is currently at that place.
   - B. The person arrived a long time ago.
   - C. The person will go there next.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — This is the past-time sense of once. It does not count the number of occurrences.
2. A — C10 handles once as one occurrence only; C11 handles the past-time sense.
3. A — The source's long-time example is expressed as a long time back.
4. A — Already describes the event as having taken place before the present point.
5. A — The source gives a separate present-location context. It is not the same as the completed-event use.

### B. Events through now, negative yet and and yet

1. An account includes events from an earlier period through the present point. Which description matches the source?
   - A. An account of what has taken place so far / up to now.
   - B. An account only of what will happen next.
   - C. An account of one event only.

2. A person is expected at a place but has not arrived by the present point. Which relation is intended?
   - A. The person is still absent.
   - B. The person is already there.
   - C. The person has arrived in the past.

3. The source's negative-yet example says that the person has not come by now. Which meaning does this test?
   - A. A past event.
   - B. An event not completed by the present point.
   - C. A repeated action.

4. A sentence begins with and yet as a joining statement. Which meaning does the source give?
   - A. Two statements are true at the same time.
   - B. An event has not happened by now.
   - C. An action will happen after this.

5. Are negative yet and and yet the same use?
   - A. Yes. Both always mean that an event has not happened.
   - B. No. One concerns an event not completed by now; the other joins statements that are true at the same time.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source uses this description for an account covering events through the present point.
2. A — Negative yet describes the expected arrival as not having happened by now.
3. B — The relation is an outstanding event at the present reference point.
4. A — The joining use of and yet is explained as at the same time.
5. B — The two contexts must not be collapsed into one translation.

### C. since — time or reason

1. The source gives an example about friends not seen since a past year and explains the complete idea as not having seen them for a long time. What must the learner remember?
   - A. This explains the whole example; it does not make since a universal substitute for for a long time.
   - B. Since always means for a long time.
   - C. Since always gives a reason.

2. Events that have taken place since a particular earlier point are described by the source as taking place:
   - A. before that point.
   - B. after that point.
   - C. at the same time as that point.

3. In the source's example about it being late and going to bed, since gives:
   - A. a reason.
   - B. a repeated action.
   - C. a nearby position.

4. Which sentence uses since as a reason rather than a time relation?
   - A. Events that happened since then.
   - B. Friends not seen since a past year.
   - C. Since it is late, it is time to go to bed.

5. Should every use of since be interpreted as a time starting point?
   - A. Yes.
   - B. No. The source also uses it to give a reason.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source paraphrases the complete friend example. It does not establish one fixed replacement for every since sentence.
2. B — Since then identifies events that took place after the earlier point.
3. A — In this context since means because.
4. C — The clause about lateness gives the reason for the action.
5. B — The learner must distinguish time from reason.

### D. next — sequence, repetition or position

1. The sentence asks what will be done next. Which relation is intended?
   - A. The action that follows in the sequence.
   - B. The same action happened in the past.
   - C. A place is nearest to another place.

2. The sentence says that a person next performs the same action. What does next indicate?
   - A. The next occasion on which the same action is repeated.
   - B. A different action that must happen first.
   - C. A place beside the person.

3. One house is next to another. Which relation is intended?
   - A. Time sequence.
   - B. Spatial closeness.
   - C. Repetition.

4. Which use of next belongs to Module 2 · Lesson 4 rather than this lesson's time/sequence teaching?
   - A. The action that follows.
   - B. The next occasion of the same action.
   - C. The house next to another house.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source explains this as the action that follows the present one.
2. A — Here next marks the next occurrence of the same action.
3. B — This is a spatial relation.
4. C — Spatial adjacency cross-links to Movement & Position.

### E. always — general meaning or a particular context

1. What is the general meaning of always given by the source?
   - A. At all times.
   - B. Only once.
   - C. After a particular event.

2. In some connections, what other sense may be intended?
   - A. Often / frequently.
   - B. Never.
   - C. Only in the past.

3. When a future relationship is specially in view, what expression does the source recommend for the example about remaining someone's friend?
   - A. for ever.
   - B. after then.
   - C. a long time back.

4. Does the source say that for ever, unchanged, and endless are universal replacements for always?
   - A. Yes.
   - B. No. The source attaches them to particular contexts and says the latter descriptions suit other connections.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — This is the general meaning.
2. A — The source says that frequently may fit some connections.
3. A — For a future relationship, the source says for ever is a better way of putting it.
4. B — These expressions are context-dependent, not interchangeable in every sentence.

### F. Keep neighbouring lessons and foundations separate

1. A sentence means that an action happened one time only. Which lesson is its primary home?
   
   **Answer:** Module 2 · Lesson 10 — Quantity, Amount & Degree.

2. A sentence means that something was true during an earlier period. Which lesson is its primary home?
   
   **Answer:** Module 2 · Lesson 11 — Time & Sequence.

3. The word next describes a place beside another place. Which lesson provides the spatial foundation?
   
   **Answer:** Module 2 · Lesson 4 — Movement & Position.

4. Since gives a reason rather than a time relation. Where does that sentence relation belong?
   
   **Answer:** The existing sentence-relation foundation. C11 identifies the contrast but does not create a separate reason lesson.

5. A learner needs to name a date, read a clock or count a period of time. Is that the main task of C11?
   
   **Answer:** No. Those are part of the existing number/time foundation. C11 focuses on the additional meanings and context distinctions in the source.

### G. Reverse understanding

Explain what relation is described by each expression.

1. Something was true during an earlier period.
2. A long time back.
3. An event happened before the present point.
4. An account includes events through the present point.
5. An expected person has not arrived by now.
6. Two statements are true at the same time.
7. Events happened after a particular earlier point.
8. The sentence gives a reason.
9. The action that follows in a sequence.
10. The next occurrence of the same action.
11. A place is spatially closest to another place.
12. Something is true at all times.
13. Something happens often in the particular context.
14. A future relationship is intended to continue without an end.

**Suggested answers**

1. Once in its past-time sense.
2. The source's Basic expression for its long-time ago example.
3. Already in the completed-event context.
4. So far / up to now.
5. Negative yet.
6. And yet as a joining statement.
7. Since then.
8. Since meaning because.
9. Next in sequence.
10. Next referring to the next occasion of the same action.
11. Next to; cross-link to C04.
12. Always in its general sense.
13. Always in a context where frequently is intended.
14. Always in a future-focused context; the source recommends for ever as a better way of putting it.

### H. Independent construction

Choose two situations and explain the time relation before constructing a Basic expression:

- something was true during an earlier period;
- an event happened before the present point;
- an account covers events through now;
- an expected event has not happened by now;
- two statements are true at the same time;
- an event happened after a stated earlier point;
- a sentence gives a reason;
- an action follows another action;
- the same action happens on the next occasion;
- something continues at all times;
- something happens frequently in a particular context;
- a future relationship is intended to continue without an end.

First identify the relation. Then choose the expression that preserves it. Finally, explain why a neighbouring meaning does not fit.
