# Module 1 · Lesson 1 — Names of Things: Meaning Expansion

> **Draft status:** source-reviewed teaching draft. It is based on C. K. Ogden's *The ABC of Basic English* (1932), especially pp.146–158 and the p.160–162 expansion exercises. The lesson uses selected teaching examples and a compact reference list; it is not a verbatim transcription of every source passage. Clarity, exercise quality and actual lesson length still require a final teaching review.

## What you will learn

A Basic Name can sometimes be used for another thing when the source recognizes a real connection between its starting meaning and the new thing.

The important point is the connection:

**starting meaning → recognizable relation → related thing**

This lesson is about meaning **expansion**: the meaning reaches out to a related referent. It is not the same as **Special Use**, where context narrows a word to a more specific kind. That is a separate lesson.

A connection must be explainable and supported by the Basic source. A personal association is not enough.

## 1. From body parts to parts of other things

Start with four familiar examples:

| Word | Starting meaning | Related use |
|---|---|---|
| head | the part of the body above the neck | the rounded top of another thing |
| foot | the part used for standing and walking | the base of another thing |
| mouth | the opening used for eating and speaking | an opening in another thing |
| neck | the part connecting the head and body | the narrow part of a bottle or violin |

The connection is based on shape, position or structure:

- A foot supports the body; the foot of another thing can be its base.
- A mouth is an opening; the mouth of another thing can also be an opening.
- A neck is a narrow connecting part; the neck of a bottle or violin has a related position and shape.

The other source-listed body-part examples are useful to recognize:

| Word | Related use in the source |
|---|---|
| arm | a branch or rod roughly in the position of an arm; the source also compares some older instruments of war to longer, stronger arms |
| leg | the supporting part of a seat or bed |
| chest | a box-like container, as in chest of drawers |
| lip | a curved outlet or edge |
| tail | the back end |
| tooth | a pointed part |
| face | the side at the front or before one's eyes |

These examples do not mean that every object has a body part. They show a source-recognized extension from a familiar body-part meaning to a related position, shape or structure.

## 2. Similarity in size, form, group or sound

The source first uses comparisons that can be recognized through the senses. Its examples include:

| Connection | Source examples | What connects them |
|---|---|---|
| size | mountains; babies | very large or very small things |
| number / group | an army | a number of people understood as one group |
| form / arrangement | plates, leaves, a basin, a stick, cakes of soap, family trees | flatness, thinness, a hollow form, a long narrow form, or a branching arrangement |
| sound | thunder; music | the sound of guns compared with thunder; the sound of a river compared with music |

The source's point is not that these things are identical. It is that a familiar Name can be extended when a recognizable feature connects the two uses.

## 3. Similarity through purpose or behavior

The source says that comparisons based on purpose or behavior are more complex, and that they form its largest and most vivid group.

Examples include:

| Source example | Connection described |
|---|---|
| bodies in space compared with the sun and moon | similar behavior and properties |
| a breath of wind and the breath we give out | a related kind of movement |
| a shoe on a horse's foot and a shoe worn by a person | a recognizable similarity between the two uses |
| the bed of a river or a flower-bed and the bed in which we rest | a longer chain of connection; not an exact identity of use |
| crimes for which a man is sent to prison and crimes for which society is responsible | a related use of the same Name in personal and social responsibility |
| an automatic machine in a station and a person called a machine because he repeats the same kind of work | similarity of behavior |
| the kick of an animal and the kick of an engine | a related action or effect |

These examples do not all share one simple formula. Do not replace them with an invented rule such as “anything that does the same job has the same name”. The learner's task is to understand the particular connection in each source example.

## 4. “As if” comparisons and abstract meanings

Some extensions use a familiar physical relation to help describe something abstract:

- **a field of interest:** the range of someone's thoughts or interests is compared with a limited space;
- **the attraction of cakes for a small boy:** cakes attract the child, but he is not physically pulled towards them like a needle towards the North Pole;
- **fixed ideas:** ideas are not physically fixed to anything in the way a picture can be fixed to a page with paste;
- **money support:** financial support does not bear weight; the source contrasts it with a church roof resting on real supports of wood or stone.

The roof and its wooden or stone supports are literal physical objects in the source's comparison. The extended uses are field of interest, attraction, fixed ideas and money support.

The purpose of an “as if” comparison is to make an abstract meaning understandable through a familiar relation. It does not give permission to describe any abstract idea with any physical word.

## 5. Other source-recognized connections

Not every expansion is based on similarity. The source also gives these relations:

| Relation | Source examples | Meaning connection |
|---|---|---|
| cause | amusement, comfort, pleasure, agreement | the Name of a feeling or thing is used for what causes it |
| process | addition, building, discovery | the Name of a thing or result is used for the process that produces it |
| use | brush, sail, whistle | the Name of an object is extended to a use or activity connected with it |
| part / whole or collective | letter, country, body | the source connects the letters making a letter, a person's country of birth, and people acting together as one body in law |
| material / object | a glass, an iron, a tin | a material Name is used for a familiar object made from it: a drinking vessel, a dress-iron, or a tin box/pot |
| other real connections | gold, orange, relation, force, library, spring, fall, summer, note, rest | colour, family relation, having force, books kept in a library, seasons, a musical sound and what remains |

The source also mentions harder connections such as receipt and directions, a woodworker's plane and an airplane, the frame of a picture and the frame of a building, and certain in different senses. It says that when a learner's own language gives no similar comparison, these harder cases can be learned first and examined again later.

**Important:** these are recorded uses, not unlimited rules. A material Name does not automatically name every object made from that material; a part does not automatically name every whole; and a personal association does not establish an accepted expansion.

## 6. How to understand an unfamiliar use

When you meet a Basic Name in a new context, ask:

1. What is the word's familiar starting meaning?
2. What thing does it refer to in this sentence?
3. What connects the two uses: shape, position, sensory feature, behavior, purpose, cause, process, use, part/whole, material/object, or another source-listed relation?
4. Can you point to a source-listed use or explain the relation clearly, rather than relying only on a personal association?
5. Is this an expansion, or is the context narrowing the word to a more specific kind?

If you cannot explain the connection, do not invent one.

## 7. The source does not require every word to have an expansion

On p.158, Ogden makes an important limit clear:

- Quite a number of words have neither an expansion nor a Special Use.
- Some words have only an expansion; others have only a Special Use.
- A word may have more than one expansion.
- The source limits a word to one Special Use in this account, to avoid confusing the learner.

So this Lesson is **not** a checklist that gives every one of the 850 words an extra meaning. It teaches how to recognize the source-supported cases. A word with no listed expansion should simply be learned in its root use.

The source uses **law** and **line** to show that one word can have several connected expansions:

| Word | Connections described by the source |
|---|---|
| law | a man-made rule; a general statement of fact; the system of laws in society; the people responsible for putting laws into effect; the organization through which they do so |
| line | the distance between two points; a long mark; things arranged side by side; a railway; a piece of cord; the ships belonging to a company |

The intended sense still depends on what is being talked about. Do not assume that all these uses fit every occurrence of the word.

## 8. Further source examples from pp.160–162

The source gives five more expansion exercises on p.160 and explains the connections on p.162:

| Word | Source example | Connection shown |
|---|---|---|
| army | birds in flight described as an army in flight | a group or formation |
| reaction | a government of reaction; a reaction against bright ideas | a response or attitude in a social/political context |
| society | two or three people forming a small society | a group of people |
| worm | a person looked down on as a poor sort of animal | an animal name used in a judgment about a person |
| wing | the side part of a building | compared with the outstretched wing of a bird |

These are source-listed examples, not general permission to create similar expressions freely.

On p.162, the source also gives an open classification exercise with **invention, jump, stage, stiff, deep, horn, net, star**, and asks where **humor** and **wash** belong. This can be used as an optional discussion task: classify only when you can explain the source-supported connection. It is not a fixed-answer automatic test.

## Practice

### A. Recognize the connection

Choose the best answer.

1. The word **mouth** is used for an opening in another thing.
   - A. A related structure
   - B. A time sequence
   - C. A promise

2. A long, thin object is described with the word **stick**.
   - A. A financial relation
   - B. A similarity of form
   - C. A past event

3. The source compares a shoe on a horse's foot with a shoe worn by a person.
   - A. A similarity between uses
   - B. A material-to-object relation
   - C. A time relation

4. A person who repeats the same kind of work all the time is called a **machine**.
   - A. A similarity of behavior
   - B. A body-part relation
   - C. A quantity relation

5. **Addition** is used for the process by which something is produced.
   - A. A process relation
   - B. A sound relation
   - C. A position relation

6. **A glass** is used for a drinking vessel.
   - A. A material-to-object relation
   - B. A movement relation
   - C. A feeling

7. A thing that causes amusement is referred to by the Name **amusement**.
   - A. A cause relation
   - B. A time relation
   - C. A material relation

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The mouth of another thing is an opening.
2. B — The source connects stick with a long, thin form.
3. A — The source treats the two shoes as a recognizable comparison.
4. A — The comparison is based on repeated behavior.
5. A — The Name is used for the process that produces something.
6. A — Glass is the material Name used for a familiar drinking vessel.
7. A — The source allows the Name of a feeling or thing to be used for what causes it.

### B. Explain the connection

Explain each pair in your own words:

1. foot → the base of another thing;
2. river-bed / flower-bed → the bed in which we rest;
3. a number of people → one body in the eyes of the law;
4. money support → the literal supports of a church roof;
5. library → the books that go in a library.

**Suggested explanations**

1. The source extends foot from the body to a base.
2. The source gives this as a longer chain of connection. The uses are related, but they are not the same object or activity.
3. People acting together can be treated as one body in law.
4. Money support is abstract help; the roof's wooden or stone supports physically bear weight.
5. The source lists library as a Name used for the books that go in it. This is a recorded connection, not a rule that every place Name can name its contents.

### C. Transfer to another source example

The source compares the kick of an animal with the kick of an engine.

What is being extended?

**Answer:** the Name for an action is used for a related action or effect produced by an engine. The engine is not an animal; the connection is the action/effect being described.

### D. Check the boundary

**Project-created contrast — not an example from Ogden:** someone calls a small chair a mountain only because both can be found outside.

Is that enough to establish a Basic meaning expansion?

**Answer:** No. Sharing a setting is only a personal association here. The source does not establish this as an accepted expansion.

### E. Reverse check

1. In **the mouth of a bottle**, what does mouth refer to?
2. In **money support**, does support mean a physical beam holding something up?

**Answers**

1. The opening of the bottle.
2. No. It means financial help. The source uses the church roof's physical supports as a contrast.

## Lesson review

You should now be able to:

- identify a Name's familiar starting meaning;
- recognize a source-supported related use in context;
- explain the connection instead of memorizing an unrelated second translation;
- distinguish a source-supported expansion from a personal association;
- distinguish expansion from Special Use / meaning narrowing.
