- The 250 Fixed Word-Groups remain phrase-level reinforcement. The 50 Most Necessary groups have all been mapped; all 250 source items now have an instance-level teaching index; the 50 Most Necessary groups are a priority subset of those 250.

## Whole-sequence progression test 01 — order, boundaries and prerequisites (2026-11-18)

### A. Module 1 progression: C13 → C14 → C15

| Transition | Learner task | Progression result |
|---|---|---|
| C13 Names of Things expansion | Recognize the root referent and the relation that extends it to another object/situation | Start with concrete object/structure relations, then move to sensory, function, process, part/whole and abstract connections |
| C13 → C14 | Move from expanded object Names to expanded Quality meanings and Quality-as-Thing use | Different word class and semantic operation; keep separate, but reuse the same “root sense → relation → contextual meaning” method |
| C14 → C15 | Move from related meaning extension to contextually narrowed reference | A real change in decision: C14 asks why a related sense is valid; C15 asks which narrower referent the context selects |

**Result:** the three-lesson sequence is coherent. C15 depends on the expansion/narrowing contrast established in C13/C14. C13 and C14 should not be merged solely to reduce count: the source material and teaching tasks have separate enough scope. No evidence in this pass supports splitting either into smaller Lessons.

### B. Module 2 progression: C01 → C12

| Lesson | Main decision taught | Why it follows here |
|---|---|---|

### C. Exact prerequisite handling — C03

The current candidate previously said “include a short be/get + past-participle result pattern.” The repository's actual Step 17 adaptation already teaches this material:

- past-participle forms of Operators;
- the distinction between an action's receiver/result and the -ing form;
- **be + past participle** passive/result construction;
- passive examples with and without an agent introduced by by;
- **get + past participle**, including “get some stockings made”, “gets food sent” and “got the work done”;
- a passive transformation exercise.

This is confirmed in docs/js/course.js under lessonHTML[17], and the Step 17 adaptation audit records passive and get + past participle as covered topics.

**Revised C03 prerequisite:**

1. Prerequisite/reference: completed App Step 17, sections 4–6 (past participle, be-passive, get + past participle).
2. C03 should provide a brief reminder using its own source examples (“the thread is broken”; “get it broken”; “the plate may be smashed”; “it comes to bits”).
3. Do **not** reteach the full passive/get-passive rule and do not create another foundation Lesson.
4. If a learner has not completed Step 17, the C03 lesson should link back to that foundation section before the result-form exercise.

This resolves the earlier statement that Step 17 was not locally adapted: Step 17 **is** adapted in the current App. Step 22 is not locally adapted, but it is not the only relevant source of passive teaching.

### D. Exact prerequisite handling — C09

The source-derived Other Suggestions index gives C09 enough material to teach its own distinctions. It must not assume that the learner already knows a dedicated modal unit.

Teach the source's separate uses:

- **can**: ability / possibility;
- **ought**: what is right or wise; what is someone's business/duty; and a probability inferred from the facts;
- **shall** in a statement: will;
- **Shall I ...?**: asking about intention (“Am I going to do it or not?”) or whether doing it is wise.

**Revised C09 prerequisite:**

1. Basic sentence patterns, Operators and infinitive forms from the existing foundation.
2. Teach the above can/ought/shall meanings inside C09 itself.
3. Keep **unless → if ... not** outside C09; it belongs to conditional clause/sentence-structure teaching.
4. Do not assume Step 21/22 auxiliary material is already available in the local App; the source-specific C09 meanings are still taught here.

C09 is therefore self-contained for its target meaning distinctions, while relying on the already-taught sentence foundation.

### E. Remaining scope warning — C07

C07 was initially the smallest candidate Lesson.

The directly indexed Some Common Needs material is buy, sell, keep goods for sale and promise to pay later. The priority fixed-group list supplies the directly related “let a house” use, but most of the other 50 priority groups do not belong to exchange/sale and must not be forced into C07.

The remaining 200 of the 250 Fixed Word-Groups are not individually indexed. Therefore:

- C07 remains a **provisional compact Lesson**, not yet proven to have the same teaching size as the others;
- do not pad it with unrelated social/relationship phrases;
- after the remaining 200 fixed groups are indexed, recheck whether they contain source-supported exchange/sale/commitment material that naturally belongs here;
- if not, test whether C07 should be a shorter subsection of a broader transaction/relationship theme rather than a standalone Lesson.

This is the only adjacent-scope/lesson-size concern that remains material after this progression pass. The current evidence does not justify changing the 15-count yet.

### F. Cross-cutting and non-duplication check

- Operators / Directives: prerequisite tools, not repeated as separate theory in every Lesson.
- Names of Acts: existing foundation; C01–C08 deepen semantic choices and context.
- Names of Directions: existing foundation; C02–C04 apply spatial relations in physical contexts.
- Step 17: C03 prerequisite for passive/result forms, with a short local reminder only.
- Meaning → Basic: shared method; each Lesson trains its own semantic choice rather than re-teaching the method.
- Fixed Word-Groups: insert only where meaning naturally reinforces the current topic.
- -er / -ing / -ed: retain as word-form foundation; do not confuse derived-word morphology with passive/result syntax.
- C12 vs C15: C12 starts from a non-Basic ordinary-English word and constructs a Basic paraphrase; C15 starts from a Basic word and interprets a contextually narrowed use. The direction of the learner's task is different.

## Why there are only two candidate Modules

### Not a separate Fixed-Word-Group Module

The 250 Fixed Word-Groups and the 50 Most Necessary groups occur across:

- movement;
- state;
- attitude;
- modality;
- time;
- social relations;
- process;
- memory;
- causation;
- etc.

They are therefore a **reinforcement layer**, not a coherent learning task of their own.

### Not a separate Endings Module inside this Expression course

The -er / -ing / -ed material is a reusable word-form mechanism.

Its examples appear in physical action, communication, learning, states and many other topics. The productive rule belongs to the basic word-form foundation; important derived meanings should be reinforced where they become useful.

### Not a separate Operator/Directive Module

Operators, Directives, word forms and sentence structure are foundational tools already handled by the Basic English teaching/45-Step side of the project.

The Expression System should **use those tools to solve meaning problems**, not duplicate the foundation.

**2 candidate Modules / 14 candidate Lessons.**

This number is **not final**.

The 14 are the smallest current set that gives each clearly different learning task a visible home without turning every source subsection or individual word into a Lesson.

## Stress points that must still be tested

### Stress point 1 — Lesson 02

“eat/bite/spit” and “touch/hit/scratch” are related physical actions but not identical. The candidate combines them because both train the learner to describe bodily contact/action using Basic components. This needs a lesson-size test.

### Stress point 2 — Lesson 04

Object transfer and body posture are different decision problems. The candidate combines them because both are movement/position material and strongly reuse the same source/destination/location tools. This is a deliberate compression that must be tested.

### Stress point 3 — Lesson 07

Learning/teaching is coherent; show is polysemous; try is currently a supporting pattern. This Lesson may need restructuring after practice design.

### Stress point 4 — Lesson 08

The direct Some Common Needs source material is relatively small. It needs the fixed-word-group and participation/relationship material to be tested before being accepted as a standalone Lesson.

### Stress point 5 — Lesson 10 / 11

Modality, condition, quantity, degree and time are all function-oriented expressions but do not share exactly the same semantic task. The current grouping is only a candidate compression and must be tested.

### Stress point 6 — Module 2

The boundary between:

- expansion;
- specialization;
- quality↔thing;
- contextual special sense

may support 2 Lessons instead of 3, or require a different division. This needs actual exercise design before final counting.

## Acceptance rule for v0.1

The candidate structure can become the final structure only if every Lesson passes all of these tests:

1. It has one dominant learner decision problem.
2. Its source examples form enough material for meaningful practice.
3. It does not duplicate a foundation lesson.
4. Fixed groups and endings can be inserted naturally without distorting the Lesson.
5. A learner can move from meaning → Basic construction → reverse interpretation.
6. Adjacent Lessons have a visible reason for being separate.
7. No Lesson exists merely because the source has a heading or because a target count needs another item.

Until those tests are passed, **14 is a candidate count, not a project commitment.**

### 2. Important source-classification corrections

These corrections matter more than the current Lesson count:

1. **“Busy” and “deaf” are Other Suggestions expression examples.** Their task is to describe an ordinary-English Quality with Basic words (e.g. “working hard / has much to do”; “whose hearing is bad”). They are not Names of Qualities meaning-expansion examples.
2. **“World”, “life”, “hill”, “home” and “address” are also Other Suggestions.** They require context-sensitive expression decisions. They must not automatically be placed in the Names of Things special-use/narrowing lesson.
3. **Qualities used as Names of Things** is a word-class/function boundary. It is not the same as Special Use / narrowing. Link it to the existing word-class foundation and include it in a semantic lesson only if the source examples require it.
4. **“Unless”** is the conditional pattern “if ... not”; it should not be grouped with modal meanings merely because both occur in Other Suggestions.
5. **The 50 priority fixed groups are reinforcement material, not a reason to create a “social relations” lesson.** For example, “take part in”, “one another”, “respect for”, “dependent on” and “let a house” have different natural uses. Insert each where its meaning belongs.
6. The “special use” discussed in Names of Things and Qualities (meaning narrowing) must not be confused with the 250 Fixed Word-Groups section's special/idiomatic uses.

The second, misplaced row has been removed. The example remains once under physical change. The movement/posture group therefore has **14 unique indexed rows**, not 15.

## Teaching-size test 01 — Bodily action/contact vs physical effects/result

The purpose of this test is to see whether candidate 02 and 03 can be taught as two short, natural units with different decisions, rather than merely splitting a source paragraph.

### Candidate 02 — Bodily actions and contact

**Teaching objective:** identify what bodily action is happening and what relation between the body, the object and the action must be made explicit.

Two internal sections are needed:

**A. Intake, biting and expulsion**

- eat → take food / have a meal
- bite a piece → have a bite of it / at it
- spit food out → put it out of your mouth

**B. Contact, impact and rubbing**

- touch something → put a hand/finger on it / give it a touch
- touch by chance → come up against it
- hit something hard → give it a blow
- scratch with a pointed object → make a mark on it
- scratch an insect bite → give it a rub
- scratch deeply → get one's nails into it

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. Someone is eating a meal. Choose between “take food” and “have a meal” according to what the sentence emphasizes.
2. Someone bites a piece off an apple. Express the action with “have a bite”.
3. Someone spits food out. Preserve the outward direction and body location: “put it out of your mouth”.
4. Someone touches a wall to test whether it is dry. Express the body part and contact relation: “put a hand on it”.
5. Someone hits an object hard. Express the impact as “give it a blow”.
6. Someone scratches an insect bite to reduce irritation. Do not use “make a mark”; the source gives “give it a rub”.
7. Someone scratches a surface with a pointed object. Here the relevant result is “make a mark”.

**Test result:** this can be taught as one compact bodily-action theme if the two sections remain explicit. The common task is identifying the actual bodily event and the body/object relation; it is **not** a single reusable grammar formula. It is therefore retained as one candidate Lesson for now, but it must not be described as one uniform construction.

### Candidate 03 — Physical effects: damage, change and result

**Teaching objective:** distinguish an action that changes or damages something from the resulting state, and from a change that happens by itself.

**Source-grounded contrasts:**

- cut a person → give the person a cut;
- cut through thread or cake → get it cut;
- break a thread → the thread is broken / get it broken;
- smash a plate → the plate may be smashed;
- a plate breaks by itself → it comes to bits;
- destroy something → burn it / smash it / break it / put an end to it;
- hurt someone → give them pain / give wounds / do serious damage.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. A person cuts another person. Express the injury event, not the physical route of a cutting tool.
2. A thread is cut through. Distinguish the affected object and the result construction.
3. A thread is already broken. Describe the resulting state rather than the action that caused it.
4. A plate is smashed by someone. Keep the agent-caused result distinct from the plate breaking by itself.
5. A plate breaks by itself. Use “come to bits”, not the agentive “give it a blow”.
6. Someone hurts another person. Choose whether the context concerns pain, wounds or serious damage.

**Test result:** this is a genuinely different teaching task from candidate 02. The learner must distinguish action, caused result, resulting state and self-occurring change. The source itself marks cutting/breaking as operations connected with changes of form. Keep this as a separate candidate Lesson and rename it **Physical Effects: Damage, Change & Result**.

### Boundary decision after Teaching-size test 01

- Candidate 02 remains one provisional Lesson with two explicit subtopics: intake/expulsion and contact/force.
- Candidate 03 remains a separate Lesson because it teaches action/result/state distinctions that candidate 02 does not.
- The boundary is based on the learner's decision, not the source's page break.
- This test does not yet establish the final total Lesson count.


## Teaching-size test 02 — Movement, transfer and body position

### Candidate 04 — Movement and Position: Transfer, Posture & Viewpoint

**Teaching objective:** describe where a person or thing is, where it moves, and how the movement/position relates to a reference point. The learner must distinguish transfer/path, held position, posture change and the speaker's viewpoint.

Two internal sections are needed.

**A. Moving, sending and holding an object**

Source examples:

- throw a ball to a baby → send the ball to a baby;
- throw over a wall / across a river → send the ball over / across;
- a blow pushes someone against a table → the blow sends the person against the table;
- catch a ball → get it in the hand;
- hold a ball → get a grip of it / keep it / have it in the hand;
- bring delicate things → take them / come with them, depending on viewpoint.

The central decision is: **what moves, what is the destination/path, and from whose viewpoint is the movement described?**

**B. Position and change of posture**

Source examples:

- lie on something → be on it / rest on it;
- stand up → get up;
- sit → take a seat / be seated.

The central decision is: **is the sentence describing an existing position, or the action of moving into that position?**

**Supporting meaning contrasts**

- run quickly → go quickly;
- run for health → take a run;
- run away from danger → go (off) at a run;
- slip → have a slip / have a fall.

These examples are useful because they show that one ordinary word can describe speed, purposeful activity or escape, and that an accidental event can be expressed as a Name. They do not need to become independent Lessons.

**Additional source examples for path practice**

- take your arm out of your coat;
- put money into your pocket;
- go quickly out of the house.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. A ball is thrown across a river. Preserve the path: “send the ball across the river”.
2. A child catches a ball. Express the new position: “get it in the hand”.
3. A person keeps a ball in their hand. Compare “keep it”, “have it in the hand” and “get a grip of it”; do not treat them as identical in every context.
4. A person is bringing eggs toward the speaker. Decide between “take them” and “come with them” from the speaker's viewpoint.
5. A person is already sitting. Use the state expression “be seated”; when telling someone to sit, use the action expression “take a seat”.
6. A person stands up from lying down. Express the change of posture: “get up”.
7. Compare “run quickly”, “run for health” and “run away from danger”. Do not give all three the same Basic rendering.
8. Someone slips and falls. Express the event with “have a slip / have a fall”.

### Test result

The two internal sections ask different local questions, but both belong to the same spatial theme:

- object: movement, path, destination, holding and viewpoint;
- person: position, movement into a position, and posture.

The examples are sufficient for one coherent lesson if the objective stays at **movement and position relative to a reference point** and the two sections remain explicit. The run/slip examples are supporting contrasts, not the centre of the lesson.

**Decision:** retain candidate 04 as one provisional Lesson, with two sections. Do not split object transfer and posture into separate Lessons at this stage. Reconsider only if the full lesson prototype becomes too long or the learner tasks cannot be taught in one progression.



## Teaching-size test 03 — Thinking/knowledge vs learning/teaching; the different meanings of “show”

### Candidate 05 — Thinking, Knowledge, Meaning, Memory and Learning

The current thinking/knowledge/memory set has 11 indexed examples. The separate learning/teaching set contributes three more:

- learn facts → get facts into one's head / get knowledge of them;
- be taught → be taught;
- teach a subject → give training or teaching.

These three fit the same broad knowledge theme:

- thinking/judging: what view or opinion a person has;
- knowing/understanding/meaning: what knowledge or sense a person has;
- remembering/forgetting: what is kept in or put out of the mind;
- learning/teaching: how knowledge is acquired or given.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. “I think this is so.” Choose a thought/opinion construction, not a knowledge construction.
2. “I know this fact.” Choose “be certain of it” or “have knowledge of it” according to the intended sense.
3. “I understand the argument.” Use “see the point of the argument”.
4. “I remember the fact.” Compare “have a memory of it” and “keep it in mind”.
5. “I learned the facts.” Express the acquisition of knowledge: “get the facts into one's head”.
6. “He taught the subject.” Express the giving of teaching/training.
7. “He was taught.” Preserve the passive relation; do not invent a different Basic rendering merely to avoid repeating the ordinary word.

**Test result:** the learner is still identifying a mental/knowledge relation, but the relation can be a state (know), an operation (think), retention (remember), acquisition (learn) or transfer (teach). These form a coherent knowledge theme with enough indexed material (14 examples) for one lesson with short subsections.

**Decision:** merge learn/teach into candidate 05. Retitle the candidate **Thought, Knowledge & Learning** or **Thinking, Knowledge, Memory & Learning**. Do not keep candidate 07 as a separate learning/teaching Lesson.

### The different meanings of “show” should remain together

The source gives four distinct meanings:

- show the effect of the past on the present → make it clear;
- show pictures → let someone see them / put them on view;
- show old buildings → take someone round;
- show interest → give signs of it.

And one related attempt:

- try to show an interest → make an attempt to give signs of interest.

The important point is that these are **different meanings of the same ordinary English word**. The project rule says to compare those meanings together, not scatter isolated “show = ...” answers into separate lessons.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. A speaker wants to show how the past affects the present. The intended action is making the relation clear.
2. A person shows pictures. The intended action is making the pictures visible to someone.
3. A guide shows visitors old buildings. The intended action is taking the visitors round.
4. A person shows interest. The intended action is giving signs of an attitude.
5. A person tries to show interest. The expression must retain both the attempt and the signs of interest.

These five prompts expose why “show” has no single Basic replacement. They form a compact contrast set, but not yet a full independent Lesson.

**Decision:** keep all show senses together as a multi-meaning subsection in the Communication & Information theme, with a cross-link from “show interest” to the Feeling/Attitude theme. Keep “try” as part of the final example / the supporting “make an attempt” pattern, not as a standalone Lesson.

### Boundary decision after Teaching-size test 03

- Candidate 05 absorbs learn/teach and remains one provisional Lesson.
- Candidate 07 is removed as an independent Lesson.
- All show meanings stay together in one comparison set under Communication & Information; “show interest” links to Feeling/Attitude but is not duplicated as a separate show lesson.
- “Make an attempt” remains a supporting expression pattern, not a separate course unit.


## Teaching-size test 04 — Exchange/commitment and state/process

### Candidate 08 — Exchange, Sale & Commitment

The broad source grouping contained 18 rows, but only five of them belong to a transaction/commitment theme:

- buy things → get them in exchange for money / at a price; give money for them;
- promise to pay later → give one's word to do so;
- sell things → get money for what one gives;
- keep goods for sale → keep them in a store;
- sell what one makes → put it on the market.

A directly relevant priority fixed group is:

- let a house — the fixed use for renting a house/property out.

The remaining source rows do not belong here:

- find / lose / discover → location or knowledge;
- destroy / hurt → physical damage/effect;
- grow / sleep / die / begin / finish → state and process.

**Teaching objective:** express a transaction by identifying what each party gives or gets, what the exchange is for, and whether the sentence describes a sale, goods held for sale, or a commitment to pay.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. A person buys a book. Identify what the buyer gets and what money is given in exchange.
2. A person sells a book. Reverse the roles: what does the seller get for what is given?
3. A shop keeps goods for sale. Express the goods' status and location.
4. A maker sells what they make. Express putting the goods on the market.
5. A person promises to pay later. Preserve the commitment: “give one's word to do so”.
6. A person lets a house. Recognize the fixed use; do not interpret “let” here as the general permission pattern.

**Test result:** the narrowed five-example group has one clear transaction/commitment decision and can support a short lesson. The “let a house” fixed group reinforces the rental/transaction context. The wider social-relation fixed groups (“one another”, “respect for”, “take part in”, “dependent on”) do not belong in this lesson and must be inserted where their own meanings are taught.

**Decision:** retain candidate 08 provisionally, but rename it **Exchange, Sale & Commitment**. It is not a general social-relations lesson.

### Candidate 09 — State and Process: Change, Entry & Ending

The source's ordinary-English examples concern grow / be growing, sleep, die, begin and finish. The Basic expressions actually supplied in the passage are:

- grow taller / higher / fatter / greater → get taller / higher / fatter / greater;
- be growing → be in the process of growth / development;
- sleep → go to sleep / be in sleep;
- die → go to one's death / come to one's end / take one's last breath;
- begin → make a start.

**Important source boundary:** p.256 says that everything which begins will later finish, but this is explanatory English. It does not give a Basic rendering for the general verb finish. The current 850-word list also does not contain finish. Do not teach finish → finish as a valid Basic mapping.

These examples can be organized around one process question:

> **Is the sentence describing a change in Quality, a process under way, entry into a state, a state itself, an event occurring, or an ending?**

The priority fixed groups provide natural reinforcement:

- in motion;
- in use / operation;
- go to sleep;
- lights go out;
- come to a stop;
- take place;
- put a stop to.

**in the end** is primarily a C11 Time & Sequence item. Cross-link it to C08 only when the context emphasizes the eventual outcome of a process.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples and priority groups:**

1. Someone becomes taller. Use “get taller” to express the change.
2. Someone is in the middle of growing. Distinguish “be in the process of growth” from “get taller”.
3. A child goes to sleep. Express entry into the state with “go to sleep”.
4. A person is already asleep. Identify the source's state expression “be in sleep”.
5. A machine is operating. Describe its state with “in use”.
6. A moving object reaches a stopped state. Use “come to a stop”.
7. Someone causes an activity to end. Use “put a stop to it”.
8. An event occurs. Use “take place”.
9. A person begins a task. Use “make a start”.
10. A person finishes a task. Does p.256 give a Basic rendering for this general meaning? **No.** Treat this as a source-boundary check, not as a construction prompt.

**Test result:** this is a coherent, practical unit if organized around state/process transitions rather than around the vague label “life processes”. The learner compares change, ongoing process, entry into a state, occurrence and process boundaries. The fixed groups reinforce the same theme without being treated as a separate module. Generic “finish” is not a taught Basic mapping; p.256 uses it only in explanatory English.

**Decision:** retain candidate 09 provisionally and rename it **State and Process: Change, Entry & Ending**.

- Candidate 09 survives with the process/state-transition objective; its source examples and selected fixed groups can support one concise lesson.
- No new total Lesson count is assigned.


## Teaching-size test 05 — Other Suggestions, checked against the original English pages

The original English pages pp.256, 258, 260 and 262 have now been inspected directly. This test therefore uses the actual examples on those pages, not only the shorter project index.

### Candidate 10 — Ability, Obligation & Intention

The source groups can, ought and shall together as words used in a special way with Names of Acts.

The source distinctions are:

- can: ability (“be able to”) or possibility (“it is possible for me to do it”);
- ought: right/wise action or one's business/duty to do something;
- ought in a statement about what is expected to happen: probability based on the facts;
- shall in non-question statements: will;
- “Shall I do it?”: asking whether one is going to do it or whether it is wise to do it.

**Teaching objective:** identify the speaker's relation to the action — ability, possibility, duty/advisability, probability or intention — before selecting the Basic expression.

**Project-created test prompts based on the original page:**

1. “I can do it.” Is the meaning personal ability or possibility?
2. “It is possible for me to do it.” Express possibility without changing it into an obligation.
3. “I ought to do it.” Is the intended sense that it is right/wise, or that it is one's business to do?
4. “The train ought to be here in half an hour.” Here the source explains an expectation based on facts, not a duty imposed on the train.
5. “Shall I do it?” Distinguish asking about intention from asking whether the action is wise.

**Test result:** the three words are sufficiently related for one compact lesson because the learner is deciding what stance or relation the speaker expresses toward an action. The individual meanings must remain distinct; they are not interchangeable modal replacements.

**Decision:** retain one provisional Lesson, renamed **Ability, Obligation & Intention**. Do not include unless in this lesson.

### Candidate 11 — Quantity / Amount and Time / Sequence

The original pages put number/amount examples first, then move to time words. The learning decisions are different.

**A. Quantity, amount and degree**

The source covers:

- each → every one separately;
- altogether → total amount, or quite/completely in a different use;
- whole → all the group;
- few → a small number;
- many → a great number / quite a number;
- too many → more than enough / overmuch;
- too tired to go → so tired that one cannot go;
- ill too → ill in addition;
- once → one time only, in one use.

**B. Time and sequence**

The source covers:

- ago → a long time back / in the old days;
- already → in the past / before;
- so far / up to now → what has happened up to the present;
- yet in a negative sentence → still not / so far not;
- and yet → at the same time;
- since as a time relation → after then / for a long time, depending on construction;
- since as a reason → because;
- next → after this / again / nearest to it;
- always → at all times, sometimes frequently; for ever when future continuation is emphasized.

**Project-created test prompts:**

1. “Each person gets one.” Preserve the separate distribution to every person.
2. “Six altogether.” Express the total, not six separate events.
3. “A few people came.” Use a small-number expression; do not replace it with many.
4. “Too tired to go.” Express the degree that prevents the action.
5. “He has not come yet.” Preserve that the event has not happened up to now.
6. “Since last year.” Express a time relation.
7. “Since it is late, let us go to bed.” Here since means because, not a starting time.
8. “The house next to this one.” Here next means nearest, not the next event in time.
9. “I will always be your friend.” When future continuation is emphasized, compare always with for ever.

**Test result:** quantity/amount and time/sequence have different learner decisions. The source's placement next to each other does not make them one teaching task. Both groups have enough indexed distinctions to warrant separate short-unit prototypes; once needs to be cross-linked because it can express either one-time frequency or a past-time sense.

**Decision:** split candidate 11 into two provisional topics: **Quantity, Amount & Degree** and **Time & Sequence**. Do not preserve the combined title merely to keep the Lesson count unchanged.

### Condition: unless

The source gives one compact transformation:

- unless → if ... not.

This is a conditional-clause relation, not a modal meaning. The original 45 Steps / Teaching sentence-structure foundation is the natural place to teach the conditional construction. It can be linked from the Expression System, but one source example does not justify a standalone Lesson.

### Candidate topic — Context-dependent paraphrase of qualities and common names

The original pages group two kinds of examples:

**Qualities**

- busy → one with much business; more naturally, someone who is working hard / has much to do;
- deaf → someone whose hearing is bad.

**General names**

- world → earth / everyone / things or conditions, according to context;
- life → existence / living things / all living things / living as an activity;
- hill → slope / small mountain / highlands, depending on the landform and area;
- home → a place where one is living / being with one's family;
- address → the place where one lives / the place of business / the heading at the top of a letter.

**Teaching objective:** identify the intended meaning of an ordinary word in its sentence, then express that meaning with the appropriate Basic words. The task is not to interpret an expanded meaning of a Basic word; it is to reconstruct the ordinary-English meaning in Basic.

**Project-created test prompts:**

1. “He is busy.” Choose between “working hard” and “has much to do” according to the context.
2. “He is deaf.” Express the condition through hearing.
3. “The world is shocked.” Do not use earth; the source gives everyone.
4. “The world is changing.” Choose things or conditions, not everyone.
5. “Life has existence.” Distinguish existence from living things.
6. “The hill is steep.” Choose a suitable Basic landform description such as slope or small mountain.
7. “He is happy at home.” Distinguish a place where he lives from being with his family.
8. “Write your address.” Distinguish a private address from a business address or letter-heading.

**Test result:** these examples share a practical Meaning → Basic task: the word cannot be translated in isolation; first identify the contextual sense, then choose a Basic description. The Quality examples and general-name examples are different subgroups, but the same decision method applies. The indexed material is sufficient for a compact lesson prototype.

**Decision:** create one provisional Module 1 topic, **Context-Dependent Words & Paraphrase**, containing the two subgroups. This is not the “Special Use / Meaning Narrowing” topic from Names of Things and Qualities; it belongs to ordinary-meaning-to-Basic expression.

## Teaching-size test 06 — Module 2: Expansion vs Special Use / meaning narrowing

### Candidate A — Meaning Expansion: Names of Things & Qualities

**Teaching objective:** start from a word's root meaning, identify the specific relation recognized by the source, and explain why the word can be used for the new referent/property.

The Things examples now cover several kinds of relation:

- body-part/structure relation: head, foot, arm, leg, mouth, neck, etc.;
- sensory similarity: size, shape, surface, sound;
- use/behavior/function: carriage, bed, machine, kick, etc.;
- “as if” comparison and abstract use: field of interest, attraction, support;
- cause: amusement, comfort, pleasure, agreement;
- producing process: addition, building, discovery;
- use: brush, sail, whistle;
- part and whole: letter, country, body;
- substance and object: a glass, an iron, a tin;
- other true connections: color, family relation, force, library contents, seasons, and certain historically formed relations.

Names of Qualities add a smaller but related group:

- bitter taste → bitter experience;
- cold day → cold look;
- smooth floor → smooth words;
- bright color → bright face;
- cheap → a quality associated with poor taste;
- simple → an act that requires little effort or trouble.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. “The head of a table.” Identify the relation to the human head; do not treat it as an unrelated dictionary meaning.
2. “A river-bed.” Identify the relation to the place where a river flows, rather than relying only on shape.
3. “A building.” Distinguish the process of producing the structure from the completed structure.
4. “A brush.” Identify the relation between the object name and its use.
5. “A glass of water.” Identify the substance-to-object use described on p.154.
6. “A bitter experience.” Explain the human reaction that links it to bitter taste.
7. “A cold look.” Explain the extension from physical temperature to a person's expression/attitude.
8. “A simple act.” Explain the associated low-effort / low-trouble sense, not just the root meaning of simple.

**Quality used as a Name of Things:** p.160 lists many Quality words that can function as Names of Things, with restrictions for some words (e.g. some occur only with “the”). This is a word-class/function boundary. It can be a short section or cross-link within this expansion lesson, but it is not Special Use / meaning narrowing and not a universal rule that every adjective can become a noun.

**Test result:** Things and Qualities differ in the kinds of semantic relation, but the learner performs the same core operation: identify the root sense and explain the source-supported relation to the new use. The current Quality expansion list is small; separating it into a standalone Lesson would divide the same learning operation mainly by word class.

**Decision:** test them together as one provisional **Meaning Expansion: Things & Qualities** Lesson, with separate sub-sections for Things, Qualities and Quality-as-Thing. The full source material is now much larger than the earlier four-category summary, so the lesson must be designed concisely; do not turn every relation type into its own Lesson.

### Candidate B — Special Use / Meaning Narrowing

The source explicitly distinguishes this from expansion: Special Use gives a word a **narrower**, not wider, sense.

The source examples form three useful groups:

1. A material name used for a particular piece/object formed from that material: card, chalk, cloth, paper.
2. An important subclass with its own name: shoes/boots; watches/clocks.
3. A contextually expected or default narrower sense: ball (sports), business man, judge, man (male), pictures (motion-pictures). The curtain example warns that a special use is not necessarily the most common use.

**Project-created test prompts based on the source examples:**

1. Does “a paper” mean the material in general, or a particular piece/object made from it?
2. Does “boots” name every kind of shoe, or a particular subclass?
3. In a sports context, what does “ball” refer to when no other detail is given?
4. Does “man” in this special use mean all human beings or a male person?
5. Does “pictures” in this context mean still images or motion-pictures?
6. Is the stage curtain automatically the most common meaning of curtain in everyday life? The source says no.

**Test result:** this is a different decision from expansion. The learner is not finding a relation that widens the word's meaning; the learner is using context to identify a narrower referent within a broader category.

**Decision:** retain **Special Use / Meaning Narrowing** as a separate provisional Lesson from Meaning Expansion. Do not call it simply “contextual meaning”, because that would blur it with Other Suggestions' ordinary-English-to-Basic paraphrase task and with the fixed-word-group chapter's “special uses”.

## Teaching-size test 07 — Reassess Module 1 after the complete p.154 / p.160 source review

The earlier decision to combine Names of Things and Names of Qualities into one Lesson was made when the p.154 scan was unread and the p.160 Quality-as-Thing list had not been fully incorporated into the size test. The complete source review changes the evidence, so that earlier merge must be reconsidered.

### A. Names of Things expansion

The current source index now contains several substantial relation families:

1. body-part / structure / position;
2. sensory similarity;
3. use, function and behavior;
4. “as if” comparison and abstract use;
5. cause and producing process;
6. use of an object name for its function;
7. part and whole;
8. substance and an object formed from it;
9. other source-recognized connections such as color, family relation, force, library contents and seasons.

**Teaching objective:** identify the root referent and the source-supported relation that allows the Name of Things to be used for another referent.

**Test outline:**

- Part 1: body/shape/position and structural relations (head, foot, leg, mouth, neck, etc.).
- Part 2: sensory, use and behavior relations.
- Part 3: cause, process, part/whole, substance/object and other true connections.
- Part 4: the “as if” examples and abstract uses.

This is one learner operation, but the source material is large. The lesson should teach representative examples from each relation family and keep the full source-indexed examples in the reference table. It should not become a separate Lesson for every relation type.

### B. Names of Qualities expansion and Quality-as-Thing

The complete source has two related but distinct subgroups:

**Meaning extension of Qualities:**

- bitter taste → bitter experience;
- cold day → cold look;
- smooth floor → smooth words;
- bright color → bright face;
- cheap → a quality associated with poor taste;
- simple → an act requiring little effort/trouble.

**Qualities used as Names of Things (p.160):**

The source lists 35 Quality forms that can also function as Names of Things, plus color names. It adds restrictions: dark, first, last, quiet and same occur only with “the”.

**Teaching objective:** recognize how a Quality word can (1) extend to a related property/experience, and (2) function as a Name of Things in the source-listed cases.

This is more than the six expansion examples previously counted. The p.160 list provides substantial additional material, and the two subgroups are both about the semantic/function range of Quality words. They can form one compact Lesson with two sections.

### C. Special Use / Meaning Narrowing

This remains separate:

- material names used for particular pieces/objects (card, chalk, cloth, paper);
- important subclasses (shoes/boots; watches/clocks);
- contextually expected narrower senses (ball, business man, judge, man, pictures);
- the curtain example, which warns that a special use is not necessarily the most common use.

Its decision is different: context selects a narrower referent, rather than extending the root meaning.

### Result of the size test

The revised Module 1 should contain **three** candidate Lessons, not two:

1. **Names of Things — Meaning Expansion**;
2. **Names of Qualities — Meaning Expansion & Use as Things**;
3. **Special Use — Meaning Narrowing**.

This is not a return to the original arbitrary split. The new evidence is that Things expansion has a broad set of relation families, while Qualities has both extension examples and a substantial source-listed Quality-as-Thing group. Each Lesson now has a distinct teaching objective and enough material to justify a separate unit.

The earlier v0.2 decision to merge Things and Qualities is superseded. The overall candidate count increases by one, from 14 to **15 provisional Lessons**. This is still a working count, not final acceptance.
