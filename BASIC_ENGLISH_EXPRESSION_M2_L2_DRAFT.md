# Module 2 · Lesson 2 — Bodily Action & Contact

> **Draft status:** first complete teaching-content draft. It is based on Ogden's *The ABC of Basic English*, pp.245–247. The source examples are kept distinct from project-created contexts and exercises. The lesson has two explicit parts: intake/biting/expulsion, and contact/impact/rubbing. Clarity, exercise volume and actual learner time still require review.

## What this lesson teaches

When an ordinary-English verb describes a physical action, first identify what the body is doing and what happens between the body and the object.

This lesson has two parts:

1. taking food in, biting and putting something out of the mouth;
2. touching, accidental contact, hitting and scratching.

These are related bodily actions, but they are not one grammar rule. The Basic expression changes with the actual action and the body/object relation.

## 1. Taking in, biting and putting out

The source begins with eating, then gives more specific examples of biting and spitting.

| Ordinary English meaning | Basic expression from the source | What the expression makes clear |
|---|---|---|
| eat | take food / have a meal | Food is taken in; the expression may name the action or the meal |
| be given more food than one has room for | be unable to get through it | The person cannot finish the food |
| bite a piece / bite off a bit | have a bite of it / have a bite at it | The action is expressed through the Name **a bite** |
| bite something very hard | bite it hard / get one's teeth into it | The source describes a strong physical bite |
| spit food out | put it out of your mouth | The outward direction and the body location are made explicit |

A few distinctions matter:

- **take food** and **have a meal** describe eating. They do not have to be used as exact substitutes in every sentence; choose the one that fits what is being emphasized.
- **have a bite of it / at it** uses the Name **a bite** to express the action. The source gives both forms.
- **put it out of your mouth** is more explicit than simply saying spit: it identifies what is moved, the direction, and the place it leaves.
- **be unable to get through it** is a supporting example about finishing a meal, not the main expression for eat. It links to the later State and Process lesson.

The source also uses **get your teeth into it** as a vivid description of biting hard. In this passage it is a literal physical action; do not confuse it with other figurative uses of the same English phrase.

## 2. Contact, impact and rubbing

The source then explains several meanings of touch and scratch. The important point is that these actions are not interchangeable.

| Ordinary English meaning | Basic expression from the source | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| touch something with a hand, finger or another body part | put a hand / finger / another part of the body on it | The body part is placed on the object |
| touch something | give it a touch | The contact is expressed as a small action or event |
| touch something by chance | come up against it | Contact happens accidentally |
| touch something very hard / hit it | hit it / give it a blow | The contact is forceful |
| scratch a surface with a pointed object | make a mark on it | The scratch leaves a mark |
| scratch an insect bite | give it a rub | The action is rubbing the place |
| scratch deeply with the nails | get one's nails into it | The nails enter the object or skin |

Do not treat all of these as synonyms:

- **put a hand on it** describes where the body part is placed;
- **come up against it** describes accidental contact;
- **give it a blow** describes forceful impact;
- **make a mark on it** describes the result of scratching a surface;
- **give it a rub** describes rubbing an insect bite;
- **get one's nails into it** describes a deeper scratch.

The ordinary word **touch** therefore does not always lead to one Basic expression. The context tells us whether the action is simple contact, accidental contact or forceful contact.

## 3. Boundaries with the next physical-action lessons

The original passage moves from bodily contact to cutting and breaking, then to movement and body position. Keep these learning tasks separate.

### Cut and physical damage — next lesson

The source briefly says that if a sharp blade is used instead of nails, a person may **cut her** or **give her a cut**. This is a boundary example.

The source then moves to cutting thread or cake, getting something cut, a thread being broken, a plate being smashed, and a plate that breaks by itself coming to bits. These examples focus on damage, change and result. They belong to **Module 2 Lesson 3 — Physical Effects: Damage, Change & Result**, not the core of this lesson.

### Throw, catch, hold and body position — later lesson

On p.248 the source moves to throwing or sending things, catching and holding them, bringing delicate objects, and the positions of the body. These are about movement, transfer, path, holding and posture. They belong to **Module 2 Lesson 4 — Movement & Position**, not C02.

The boundary is based on the learner's decision:

- C02: what bodily action or contact is happening?
- C03: what damage, change or result is caused, or what change happens by itself?
- C04: where does something move, where is it held, or what position is the body in?

## 4. How to choose the expression

When you meet a physical-action verb, ask:

1. **What is the body doing?** Eating, biting, spitting, touching, hitting or scratching?
2. **What is the body part?** Mouth, teeth, hand, finger or nails?
3. **What happens to the object?** Is it taken in, put out, touched, hit, marked, rubbed or scratched?
4. **How does contact happen?** Directly, by chance or with force?
5. **Is the main point actually the result or movement?** If the sentence is about damage/change, check Lesson 3; if it is about path/transfer/posture, check Lesson 4.

Do not start by applying one formula to every action. Identify the event first, then choose the Basic expression that describes it.

## Practice

### A. Identify the bodily action

Choose the expression that matches the event.

1. A person is eating a meal.
   - A. have a meal
   - B. give it a blow
   - C. come up against it

2. A person bites off a small piece.
   - A. put it out of your mouth
   - B. have a bite of it
   - C. make a mark on it

3. A person spits food out.
   - A. give it a rub
   - B. get one's nails into it
   - C. put it out of your mouth

4. A person touches an object by chance.
   - A. come up against it
   - B. have a bite of it
   - C. give it a blow

5. A person hits an object very hard.
   - A. put a finger on it
   - B. give it a blow
   - C. go through it with care

6. A person scratches a surface with a pointed object.
   - A. make a mark on it
   - B. give it a rub
   - C. have a meal

7. A person scratches an insect bite to reduce irritation.
   - A. get it cut
   - B. give it a rub
   - C. send it across the river

8. A person scratches deeply with the nails.
   - A. get one's nails into it
   - B. have a bite of it
   - C. take a seat

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source describes eating as taking food or having a meal.
2. B — A bite is used as a Name for the action.
3. C — The Basic expression states that the food is put out of the mouth.
4. A — The source uses come up against for touching something by chance.
5. B — A blow expresses forceful contact.
6. A — The source connects scratching with a pointed object to making a mark.
7. B — The source uses give it a rub for scratching an insect bite.
8. A — The source uses get one's nails into it for a deep scratch.

### B. Distinguish kinds of contact

Explain the difference between these expressions:

1. put a hand on it;
2. come up against it;
3. give it a blow;
4. make a mark on it;
5. give it a rub.

**Suggested explanations**

1. A body part is deliberately placed on the object.
2. The contact happens by chance.
3. The contact is forceful.
4. A pointed object scratches the surface and leaves a mark.
5. The action is rubbing an insect bite; it is not the same as making a mark on a surface.

### C. Keep the boundary clear

1. A person cuts another person's skin with a sharp blade. The source gives **cut her** / **give her a cut**. Why is this only a boundary example here?

   **Answer:** It is an injury or physical effect. C02 focuses on the contact action; C03 develops damage and result.

2. Someone throws a ball across a river. Does this belong to C02?

   **Answer:** No. It describes movement and path, which belong to C04.

3. A plate breaks by itself and comes to bits. Does this belong to C02?

   **Answer:** No. It describes a change and result, which belong to C03.

### D. Reverse understanding

Explain the physical action described by each Basic expression:

1. have a bite of it;
2. put it out of your mouth;
3. put a hand on it;
4. come up against it;
5. give it a blow;
6. make a mark on it;
7. give it a rub;
8. get one's nails into it.

**Suggested answers**

1. Bite a piece of it.
2. Spit it out.
3. Touch it with the hand.
4. Touch it by chance.
5. Hit it forcefully.
6. Scratch its surface with something pointed.
7. Rub an insect bite.
8. Scratch deeply with the nails.

### E. Independent construction

Choose one physical-action situation from your own experience:

- taking food in or putting it out;
- biting something;
- touching something by chance;
- hitting something;
- scratching a surface or an insect bite.

Describe the event first. Then choose the Basic expression that preserves the actual action and body/object relation. Explain why a nearby expression would change the meaning.

## Lesson review

You should now be able to:

- distinguish eating, biting and putting something out of the mouth;
- distinguish direct touch, accidental contact and forceful impact;
- distinguish making a mark, rubbing an insect bite and scratching deeply;
- recognize when the topic has changed from bodily action to physical damage/result or movement/position;
- choose a Basic expression from the actual physical event rather than applying one formula to every verb.
