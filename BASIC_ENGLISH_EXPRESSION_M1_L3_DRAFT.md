# Module 1 · Lesson 3 — Special Use: Meaning Narrowing

> **Draft status:** first complete teaching-content draft. It is based on C. K. Ogden's *The ABC of Basic English* (1932), especially pp.156–157 and the special-use questions on p.160. The examples are source-listed; short sentence contexts are project-created illustrations. Clarity, exercise quality and actual learner time still require review.

## What you will learn

A word can have a broad meaning and also a **Special Use** in which context points to a narrower, more specific thing.

This is different from meaning expansion:

- **Expansion:** a word is used for a related referent beyond its starting use.
- **Special Use:** a broad meaning is restricted to a particular object, member or sense.

The word does not lose all its other meanings. The narrower use applies when the context supports it.

This lesson does not cover Quality words used as Names of Things (Lesson 2), word-form endings, or the 250 Fixed Word-Groups.

## 1. First compare expansion and Special Use

The difference can be seen with material names:

| Operation | Example | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Expansion — C13 | glass → a drinking vessel | a material Name is extended to a familiar object made from it |
| Special Use — this Lesson | paper → a paper | a material Name is used for a particular piece of that material |

The first use reaches a related object. The second selects a particular piece or usable item from the broader material.

Do not treat every use of a material Name as the same operation. The source places these examples in different groups.

## 2. A material Name used for a particular piece

The source explains that a small piece of a substance, when used for a particular purpose, can be covered by the Name of the substance.

Its examples are:

| Broad material Name | Special Use | What is meant |
|---|---|---|
| card | a card | a piece of card used as an item |
| chalk | a chalk | a piece of chalk |
| cloth | a cloth | a piece of cloth |
| paper | a paper | a sheet or piece of paper |

The word is no longer referring only to the material in general. It is referring to a particular usable piece.

This is the source's pattern. It is not a rule that every material Name can be used with “a” to name any object made from it.

## 3. An important subclass gets its own Name

Sometimes a group contains a kind that is important enough in everyday experience to have a separate Name.

The source gives two pairs:

- shoes and boots;
- watches and clocks.

These are related kinds, but the more specific Names help us distinguish them.

The point is not that every small difference needs a new word. The source is showing that some familiar and important subgroups have their own Names.

## 4. Context selects an expected narrower sense

A word may also have a Special Use that is understood when the context gives no other details or points clearly to that sense.

| Word | Special Use in the source |
|---|---|
| ball | when no other details are given, a ball for sport |
| business man | a man who gets his living by trade |
| judge | a representative of the law |
| man | a male, not a female |
| pictures | motion-pictures |
| current | electric current |
| engine | railway engine |
| rail | engine rail |
| ring | finger ring |

The first five examples are explained in the source's discussion on p.156. The last four are supplied as questions and answers on p.160. The same p.160 page also gives army, reaction, society, worm and wing as separate meaning-expansion exercises; those are not Special Use examples.

These are not new universal definitions. For example, the source's use of pictures as motion-pictures does not mean that every occurrence of picture refers to a film. Context still matters.

## 5. A warning: Special Use is not always the most common use

The source uses **curtain** to make an important point.

A curtain on a stage has a special use, but the source observes that most people have more experience with the curtains in their homes. The special stage use is not automatically the word's most important or most common meaning.

So:

- a Special Use is a recognized narrower sense;
- it does not replace the word's broader meaning everywhere;
- it is not necessarily the sense people encounter most often.

## 6. How to identify a Special Use

When you meet a word that may have a narrower sense, ask:

1. What is the broad meaning or group?
2. What specific object, person or class is being referred to?
3. Which words or situation in the sentence support that narrower choice?
4. Is this one of the source-listed Special Uses, or am I guessing?
5. If the context does not support a narrower choice, can I keep the broader meaning instead?

Do not choose a narrow sense only because it is familiar to you.

## Practice

### A. Identify the Special Use pattern

Choose the best answer.

1. **a paper**, meaning one sheet or piece of paper:
   - A. Material Name → particular piece
   - B. Meaning expansion to a related object
   - C. A word-form ending

2. **boots** as a distinct kind alongside shoes:
   - A. A time relation
   - B. An important subclass with its own Name
   - C. An “as if” comparison

3. **a ball**, when no other details are given:
   - A. The source's expected sports sense
   - B. Any round object without context
   - C. A material Name

4. **pictures**, in the source's Special Use:
   - A. Drawings or photographs in every context
   - B. Motion-pictures
   - C. A group of paper sheets

5. **ring**, in the source's p.160 example:
   - A. A sound
   - B. A finger ring
   - C. A railway part

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The material Name paper is used for a particular sheet or piece.
2. B — Boots are named as an important kind alongside shoes.
3. A — The source says that without further details, ball is understood as a ball for sport.
4. B — The source lists motion-pictures as this Special Use.
5. B — The source gives finger ring as the narrower sense.

### B. Use the context

Identify the narrower sense.

1. “The pictures began at eight.”
2. “The judge spoke before the court.”
3. “He is a man, not a woman.”
4. “Please give me a paper so I can write a note.”

**Suggested answers**

1. Motion-pictures.
2. A legal representative.
3. A male person.
4. One sheet or piece of paper.

These sentences are project-created illustrations of the source-listed uses, not quotations from Ogden.

### C. Keep the warning about curtain

A stage curtain has a Special Use, but the source says that curtains in people's homes are more familiar to most people.

Does the stage meaning become the only meaning of curtain?

**Answer:** No. The special stage sense does not replace the broader everyday meaning, and it is not necessarily the most common use.

### D. Do not narrow without evidence

Someone says:

**“There is paper on the table.”**

Can you decide from this sentence alone that the speaker means exactly one sheet of paper?

**Answer:** No. The sentence does not establish that narrower meaning. Keep the broader meaning unless more context identifies a particular sheet or piece.

### E. Reverse check

For each narrow referent, give the broader Name:

1. one sheet of paper;
2. a ball used for sport;
3. a motion-picture;
4. a finger ring.

**Answers**

1. paper;
2. ball;
3. pictures;
4. ring.

Now change the context. Does the same narrow sense still apply? If the new context does not support it, do not carry the old Special Use over automatically.

---

## Lesson review

You should now be able to:

- distinguish expansion from Special Use / meaning narrowing;
- recognize a material Name used for a particular piece;
- recognize an important subclass with its own Name;
- use context to identify a source-listed narrower sense;
- avoid treating a Special Use as the word's only or most common meaning;
- keep the broader meaning when the context does not justify narrowing.
