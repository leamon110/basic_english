## What this lesson teaches

Ordinary English often uses one short verb for a mental relation. Basic may make that relation more explicit.

Before choosing an expression, ask what the person is doing or what relation they have:

1. thinking or forming an opinion;
2. knowing or understanding something;
3. remembering or forgetting;
4. learning or teaching.

These are four parts of one knowledge theme. They are not four separate lessons because the learner performs the same main operation: identify the mental relation first, then choose the Basic construction that expresses it.

## 1. Thinking and opinion

The source distinguishes thinking as a mental activity from holding a judgment.

| Ordinary English meaning | Basic expression from the source | What is being described |
|---|---|---|
| think about something | give thought to it | The person directs thought towards it |
| be thinking | be in thought | The person is in a state of thought |
| think that something is so | be of the opinion / take the view that | The person holds a judgment about whether it is so |

Do not treat these expressions as interchangeable:

- **give thought to** describes the activity of thinking about something;
- **be in thought** describes being occupied with thought;
- **be of the opinion** and **take the view that** express a judgment or position.

The question is not only whether the person thinks. It is whether the sentence describes mental activity or a stated opinion.

## 2. Knowing and understanding

The source uses several constructions because knowing, being aware, understanding and being familiar with something are not exactly the same relation.

| Ordinary English meaning | Basic expression from the source | What is being described |
|---|---|---|
| know that something is so | be certain of it | The person is sure that it is so |
| be aware of something | be conscious of it | The person is aware of it; this is not necessarily the same as being certain |
| know facts | have knowledge or experience of them | The person has knowledge or experience of the facts |
| know a person | be a friend of theirs / have come across them | The person is familiar with or has encountered them |
| understand what one knows | be clear about it / have a grip of it | The person has clarity or a grasp of it |
| understand horses | have a good knowledge of them | The person knows a good deal about them |
| understand a friend | see his point of view | The person understands the friend's way of seeing the matter |
| understand an argument | see the point of the argument | The person understands what the argument is saying |

The source adds that **have come across them** is used more widely for books, pictures and other things one has seen. Keep this as a familiarity/encounter example. It is not the same as being certain of a fact or understanding an argument.

A useful contrast:

- **be certain of it**: you are sure it is true;
- **be conscious of it**: you are aware of it;
- **have knowledge or experience of it**: you know about it or have encountered it;
- **see the point of it**: you understand its meaning or argument.

Do not flatten all four into a generic meaning of **know**.

## 3. Meaning and memory

The source also explains how a word's meaning and a person's memory can be expressed.

| Ordinary English meaning | Basic expression from the source | What is being described |
|---|---|---|
| mean something | have the sense of it | The meaning or sense is understood |
| remember something | have a memory of it | The person has a memory of it |
| keep something in mind | keep it in mind | The thought or fact remains in the mind |
| forget something | put it out of the mind | The person removes it from the mind |
| have no memory of something | have no memory of it | The person does not retain the memory |

Remembering and forgetting form a useful contrast:

- **have a memory of it / keep it in mind**: the information remains available;
- **put it out of the mind / have no memory of it**: the information is no longer held in memory.

The source says that when we forget something, it is no longer a part of our knowledge. This is why the two expressions are taught together.

## 4. Learning and teaching

The source then describes how knowledge is acquired and given.

| Ordinary English meaning | Basic expression from the source | What is happening |
|---|---|---|
| learn facts | get facts into one's head | Facts are acquired by the learner |
| get knowledge of something | get knowledge of it | The learner acquires knowledge |
| be taught | be taught | The learner receives teaching |
| teach a subject | give training or teaching | The teacher gives instruction |

The roles are different:

- the learner **gets** facts or knowledge;
- the teacher **gives** training or teaching;
- the learner **is taught**.

This belongs in C05 because the focus is the acquisition or transfer of knowledge. C01 is about communicating information to someone; C05 is about learning, knowing and teaching.

## 5. Keep neighbouring meanings in their own lessons

Some examples from the same original source belong to other learning tasks. Keep the boundaries visible:

- **feel a wall to see if it is dry** → put one's hands on it: physical touch, C02;
- **feel pain or pleasure / be feeling angry**: internal feeling, C06;
- **show the effect of the past on the present** → make it clear: already shown in C01's short show comparison, with a knowledge/clarity link here;
- **show interest** → give signs of it: attitude/feeling, C06; do not repeat it as a C05 lesson;
- **show pictures / show old buildings**: display or guidance, retained in C01's comparison panel;
- **try / make an attempt**: a supporting pattern in the source, not a separate C05 learning task.

These boundaries prevent the source's original Complex Acts grouping from being copied as one large undifferentiated lesson.

## 6. How to choose the expression

When you meet a mental or knowledge verb, ask:

1. **Is it an activity or a judgment?** Think about something, or hold an opinion that it is so?
2. **Is it certainty, awareness or knowledge?** Being certain, being conscious, or having knowledge/experience?
3. **Is it understanding?** Being clear, having a grip, seeing a person's point of view or seeing the point of an argument?
4. **Is it meaning?** What sense does the word or statement have?
5. **Is it memory?** Keeping something in mind, having a memory, or putting it out of the mind?
6. **Is it learning or teaching?** Who gets knowledge, who gives training, and who is taught?

Do not choose a Basic expression from the ordinary verb alone. First identify the mental relation and the role of each person.

## Practice

### A. Thinking, opinion and certainty

Choose the Basic expression that fits the meaning.

1. A person directs thought towards a question.
   - A. give thought to it
   - B. have no memory of it
   - C. give training

2. A person is occupied with thought.
   - A. be in thought
   - B. get it in the hand
   - C. take a seat

3. A person thinks that a statement is true and holds that view.
   - A. be of the opinion / take the view that
   - B. put it out of the mind
   - C. give it a rub

4. A person is sure that something is true.
   - A. be certain of it
   - B. be conscious of it
   - C. have come across it

5. A person is aware of something but the sentence does not say that they are sure it is true.
   - A. be conscious of it
   - B. be of the opinion
   - C. have a memory of it

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — Give thought to describes directing thought towards something.
2. A — Be in thought describes the state of being occupied with thought.
3. A — Opinion/view expresses a judgment.
4. A — Certain expresses being sure that it is so.
5. A — Conscious expresses awareness; the source distinguishes it from certainty.

### B. Knowing and understanding

Match the situation with the source expression.

1. A person knows facts about a subject.
2. A person knows another person through friendship or having encountered them.
3. A person understands horses.
4. A person understands a friend's position.
5. A person understands the point of an argument.
6. A person understands what a word means.

**Suggested answers**

1. have knowledge or experience of them;
2. be a friend of theirs / have come across them;
3. have a good knowledge of them;
4. see his point of view;
5. see the point of the argument;
6. have the sense of it.

The source's **come across** example is about familiarity or encounter. Do not treat it as a substitute for being certain of a fact.

### C. Remembering and forgetting

Choose the expression that matches the event.

1. A person remembers a fact.
   - A. have a memory of it
   - B. put it out of the mind
   - C. give training

2. A person keeps a fact available in memory.
   - A. keep it in mind
   - B. get it in the hand
   - C. send it across the river

3. A person forgets something.
   - A. put it out of the mind
   - B. be of the opinion
   - C. take a run

4. A person has no memory of something.
   - A. have no memory of it
   - B. have a good knowledge of it
   - C. give thought to it

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — The source uses have a memory of it for remembering.
2. A — Keep it in mind describes retaining the information.
3. A — Put it out of the mind describes forgetting.
4. A — Have no memory of it describes the absence of memory.

### D. Learning and teaching

Identify who receives or gives knowledge.

1. A learner acquires facts.
2. A teacher gives instruction in a subject.
3. A learner receives instruction.

**Suggested answers**

1. get facts into one's head / get knowledge of them;
2. give training or teaching;
3. be taught.

The learner and teacher have different roles. Do not reverse who gives and who receives.
