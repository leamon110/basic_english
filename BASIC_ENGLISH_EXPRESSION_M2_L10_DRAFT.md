# Module 2 · Lesson 10 — Quantity, Amount & Degree

> **Draft status:** source-reviewed and internally reviewed teaching-content draft. It is based on *The ABC of Basic English*, printed pp.257–258, and the accepted C10 teaching flow. It does not reteach basic number names or counting from the 45-Step foundation. The source distinctions among each, altogether, whole, few, many, too and once are kept separate; the past-time sense of once is cross-linked to C11. All 30 closed practice prompts have matching answers. Actual learner time and user feedback remain unmeasured; the lesson is not user-tested or App-integrated.

## What this lesson teaches

The words **each**, **altogether**, **whole**, **few**, **many**, **too** and **once** are not entries in the 850-word list. This lesson teaches the meanings and Basic expressions the source gives for them.

The learner identifies the relation:

1. distribution to members one by one;
2. the total count or the sense of completely;
3. a group taken as a whole or a story that is complete;
4. a small number or a great number;
5. excess amount, a degree that prevents an action, or addition;
6. one occurrence only or a past-time meaning.

Number names such as one and six belong to the existing number foundation. C10 teaches the quantity/degree relation, not number names.

## 1. each — distribution to every member

The source gives this situation:

- each person in a group gets something;
- the distribution is made to every one (separately).

The important relation is **separate distribution**: the members are considered one by one.

Do not confuse this with the total count of the group. That is the quantity sense illustrated by altogether.

## 2. altogether — total count or completely

The source gives two different uses.

### A. Total count

The source uses the example of six people “altogether”. The point is the total number in the group, not a separate distribution to each person.

It then explains the result with the words “there would make six”. Treat this as the source's illustration of the total-count meaning; do not turn it into a universal word-for-word formula for every sentence containing altogether.

### B. Completely / quite

In a statement such as **altogether wrong**, altogether means **quite** or **completely** so.

This is a degree or completeness meaning, not a count.

Keep the two readings separate:

- **six of them altogether** — the total count is six;
- **altogether wrong** — quite or completely wrong.

## 3. whole — all of a group or a complete thing

The source gives two examples:

- **the whole group** → all the group;
- **the whole story** → the complete story.

The word whole can therefore point to all members of a group or to something that is complete. Use the noun and context to identify which relation is intended.

Do not treat whole as a synonym for each: each distributes to members separately; whole views the group as one complete unit.

## 4. few and many — small or great number

The source gives:

- **few** → a small number;
- **many** → a great number or quite a number.

The source says that “few men” are very frequently two or three. It does not define few as exactly two or three. When the exact number is uncertain, it is safer to say **a small number**.

The contrast is about the size of the number:

- **few** — a small number;
- **many** — a great number / quite a number.

Do not use few and many as if they described the same amount.

## 5. too — excess, disabling degree or addition

The source gives three different uses. They must not be collapsed into one translation.

### A. Excess amount

- **too many** → more than enough (the source also gives overmuch as a gloss).

This is about the amount being greater than what is needed.

### B. A degree that prevents an action

- **too tired to go** → so tired that the person is unable to go.

Here too does not merely mean “very”. The degree of tiredness is so great that the person cannot go.

### C. Addition

- **ill too** → ill in addition.

Here too means that the person is ill as well; it adds another condition.

Compare the three:

| Source example | Meaning |
|---|---|
| too many | More than enough in amount |
| too tired to go | The degree prevents the action |
| ill too | Another condition is added |

## 6. once — one time or a past time

The source gives two different meanings.

### A. One occurrence only

- **once** → one time only.

This is about the number of times something happens. It belongs in C10.

### B. True at an earlier time

When something was **once** true, the source explains it as true **at one time** or **in the past**.

This is a time meaning, not a count of one occurrence. Its primary teaching home is C11 Time & Sequence; C10 links to it so the learner does not confuse the two meanings.

## 7. How to choose the expression

When you meet one of these words, ask:

1. **each:** Are members receiving or doing something separately?
2. **altogether:** Is the sentence giving a total count, or does it mean completely/quite?
3. **whole:** Is it all the members of a group, or a complete thing?
4. **few / many:** Is the number small or great?
5. **too:** Does it mean more than enough, a degree that prevents an action, or in addition?
6. **once:** Does it mean one time only, or at one time/in the past?

Do not choose a translation from the word alone. Identify the relation in the sentence.

## Practice

### A. Distribution, total count and whole

Choose the meaning that matches the source example.

1. Each person in a group gets something. What is the important relation?
   - A. The distribution is made to every one (separately).
   - B. The whole group is one complete unit.
   - C. The number is more than enough.

2. There are six people altogether. What does altogether express here?
   - A. The total count is six.
   - B. Each person is considered separately.
   - C. The group is completely wrong.

3. A statement says that a number is altogether wrong. Which meaning is intended?
   - A. The number is counted one by one.
   - B. The number is quite or completely wrong.
   - C. The number is a small number.

4. The whole group is present. Which Basic expression does the source give?
   - A. all the group
   - B. every one separately
   - C. more than enough

5. The whole story is known. Which Basic expression does the source give?
   - A. a great number
   - B. the complete story
   - C. one time only

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — Each concerns distribution to every member separately.
2. A — Altogether gives the total count in this example.
3. B — In altogether wrong, altogether means quite or completely.
4. A — The source explains the whole group as all the group.
5. B — The source explains the whole story as the complete story.

### B. few and many

Choose the expression that preserves the amount.

1. The intended meaning is a small number of people.
   - A. a small number
   - B. a great number
   - C. more than enough

2. The intended meaning is a great number of people.
   - A. a small number
   - B. a great number / quite a number
   - C. every one separately

3. The source says few men are frequently two or three. Does this make few an exact number meaning two or three?
   - A. Yes, always exactly two or three.
   - B. No. The source says this is frequent, but recommends a small number when uncertain.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — Few is expressed as a small number.
2. B — Many is expressed as a great number or quite a number.
3. B — The source does not make few an exact number. It recommends the clearer expression a small number when the count is uncertain.

### C. too — three different meanings

Choose the meaning that matches the context.

1. There are too many people for the room.
   - A. More than enough in amount
   - B. So tired that a person cannot go
   - C. Ill in addition

2. A person is too tired to go.
   - A. The person is ill as well.
   - B. The person is so tired that they are unable to go.
   - C. There are more than enough people.

3. A person is ill too.
   - A. The person is ill in addition.
   - B. The person is too tired to go.
   - C. The number is completely wrong.

4. Do all three examples use too with the same meaning?
   - A. Yes. It always means very.
   - B. No. It can express excess amount, a degree that prevents an action, or addition.

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — Too many expresses an amount greater than enough.
2. B — Too tired to go expresses a degree that prevents the action.
3. A — Ill too means ill in addition.
4. B — The source gives three different uses; too is not a single translation.

### D. once — count or past time

Choose the meaning that matches the sentence.

1. A thing is done once, one time only.
   - A. It is done one time only.
   - B. It was true in the past.
   - C. The whole thing is complete.

2. Something that was once true was true at one time or in the past.
   - A. It happened one time only.
   - B. It was true at one time / in the past.
   - C. It happened to every member separately.

3. Which lesson is the primary home for the past-time sense of once?
   - A. C10 — Quantity, Amount & Degree
   - B. C11 — Time & Sequence

**Answers and reasons**

1. A — This is the one-occurrence meaning and belongs in C10.
2. B — This is the past-time meaning.
3. B — The past-time meaning belongs in C11; C10 keeps a cross-link to prevent confusion.

### E. Keep the foundation and neighbouring lessons separate

1. A learner needs to say the number six. Which part of the course provides number names?
   
   **Answer:** The existing number foundation in the 45 Steps. C10 does not reteach number names.

2. Someone says “the house next to this one”. Is next being used as a time word?
   
   **Answer:** No. It describes spatial adjacency and cross-links to C04 Movement & Position.

3. A person says that something was once true in the past. Which lesson is the primary home for this meaning?
   
   **Answer:** C11 Time & Sequence. C10 teaches the separate meaning once = one time only.

### F. Reverse understanding

Explain the relation described by each Basic expression.

1. the distribution is made to every one (separately);
2. six of them altogether;
3. quite or completely wrong;
4. all the group;
5. the complete story;
6. a small number;
7. a great number / quite a number;
8. more than enough;
9. so tired that the person is unable to go;
10. ill in addition;
11. one time only;
12. at one time / in the past.

**Suggested answers**

1. Each member receives something separately.
2. The total count is six.
3. The thing is completely wrong.
4. All members of the group.
5. The story is complete.
6. Few; the number is small.
7. Many; the number is great.
8. Too many; the amount is greater than enough.
9. The person's tiredness is so great that the person cannot go.
10. The person is ill as well; another condition is added.
11. Once, in the one-occurrence sense.
12. Once, in the past-time sense; this belongs primarily to C11.

### G. Independent construction

Choose one situation:

- distributing something to every member separately;
- giving the total number in a group;
- saying that something is completely wrong;
- describing all members of a group or a complete story;
- describing a small or great number;
- expressing an amount greater than enough;
- describing a degree that prevents an action;
- adding another condition;
- expressing one occurrence only;
- describing something that was true in the past.

First identify the relation. Then choose the source expression that preserves it.

## Lesson review

You should now be able to:

- distinguish separate distribution from a total count;
- distinguish altogether as total count from altogether as completely/quite;
- express a whole group and a complete story;
- distinguish a small number from a great number;
- distinguish the three uses of too;
- distinguish once = one time only from once = at one time/in the past;
- keep the past-time sense of once in C11;
- use the existing number foundation rather than repeating number names in C10.
